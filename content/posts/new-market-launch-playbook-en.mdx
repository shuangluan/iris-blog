> _The short version: the same product launching in different countries can't copy its team structure, research methods, or acquisition channels. Acquisition especially: the cheapest channel may permanently change what your community looks like._

## Context

This is the companion to [Cold-starting a content community](/posts/content-community-cold-start-supply-en). That piece covers supply. This one covers three other things you can't avoid around launch:

1. How to build the operations team
2. How to understand local users
3. Where to buy your first users

The setting is the same: an overseas visual lifestyle community launching country by country.

## Decision 1: Building the team

### The China team and the local teams do different jobs

- **The China-based team leans toward strategy and coordination**: campaigns, social media, growth, and content ecosystem, working closely with each country's vertical operators and other functions.
- **Local teams lean toward verticals**: one person owns creator acquisition and content output for one vertical. Early on, focus goes to the priority verticals; later it broadens.

### What to hire for

Must-haves on both sides:

- **A startup mindset and self-drive**: new country, new product, nobody tells you what to do next.
- **Language**: smooth communication with both the China team and the local team.
- **Taste fit**: real understanding of the look and tone the product is going for.

Local vertical operators need one more: **command of the vertical.** What that means differs by market:

- Some markets value **industry knowledge** more: someone from the fashion industry, who worked at a related company, or who is effectively a creator-level enthusiast themselves.
- Others value **network** more: someone who has done influencer marketing and brings creators with them.

Which one matters depends on how complex the local creator market is and how hard creators are to reach.

### Four ways to get new people productive fast

1. **Onboarding docs**: write down how the team works.
2. **A buddy**: pair every new hire with an experienced teammate.
3. **Daily stand-ups**: early on, align every day on what good content and good output look like.
4. **Detailed SOPs**: any work that spans several teams gets written down step by step.

## Decision 2: Understanding local users

### Coarse first, then fine

Start with desk research to build a basic picture of the market: population and income, lifestyle and spending habits, social media usage, and the overall local creator supply. Low resolution, wide coverage.

### Three research methods for three stages

| Method | Strength | Weakness | When to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colleague and friend interviews | Fastest, most flexible | Small sample, narrow backgrounds | Earliest stage, to form hypotheses quickly |
| In-depth interviews, focus groups | Rich, verbatim user voice | Slow to organize | Once you have a direction and want to know why |
| Surveys | Large samples, more reliable conclusions | Can only test clear hypotheses | After the vertical direction is roughly set |

The most common mistake is **sending a survey too early**. A survey can only confirm a hypothesis you've already formed, like "people lack a good source of restaurant inspiration." Send one before you have a direction and you get statistics that look data-backed but don't actually answer anything.

## Decision 3: Where to buy your first users

### The real difference between channels is who the users are

| Channel | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Instagram | Better at reaching women who care about lifestyle content, but lower volume and higher cost |
| TikTok | High volume, low cost, but skews young overall |
| Google | Broader; trades audience precision for scale |
| Ad networks | Reach users the big platforms miss, but quality is uneven and needs screening; better for later expansion |

### The cheapest channel may be the most expensive

This is the point I most want to share.

TikTok's volume and low cost make it look like the obvious cold-start choice. But for a lifestyle community going for a polished look, a large influx of young users **keeps shaping what the community looks like**: what they post and like feeds back into recommendations and into every later user's first impression. Adjusting ad creative barely changes the channel's underlying audience.

**When you buy users, you're also buying what your community will become.** In a cold start the community is small, so the first waves of users have an outsized effect.

### A format worth watching: boosting creator posts

TikTok Spark Ads boost creators' native posts to drive views and installs. Compared with standard in-feed ads:

- They feel more native and usually convert better.
- The post's own engagement helps ad bidding and learning.
- The creator post's organic reach keeps paying off too.

It connects naturally to supply-side creator work: posts from creators you're already paying can be boosted directly.

### Push other tactics later

- **Referral loops** need a lot of content to work. Bring people in before the content is there and they won't stay.
- **App stores**: overseas there are essentially two, and early on the main lever is ongoing store listing optimization.
- **Taking content off-platform**: repost content that performs well in-app to other platforms. It's brand building, and it also doubles as creative testing before you put money behind boosted posts.

## Takeaways you can use

1. **The China team owns strategy and coordination; local teams own verticals.** Don't have both doing the same job.
2. **For local vertical operators, figure out whether your market lacks industry knowledge or creator network.**
3. **Match research methods to the stage.** Don't rush to a survey before you have a direction.
4. **Channels differ mainly in who their users are.** The cheapest users in a cold start may permanently change your community's look.
5. **Push referral loops and app store work later.** Get content and retention solid first.
